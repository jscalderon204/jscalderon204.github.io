While software engineering positions have a multitude of benefits that are more than eye-candy to the average developer, there still remains very large career turnover in the field for a most pleasing reason. Generally, technology positions today generally retain this high rate of turnover, but not for the reasons that one might expect. Daniel Heller, in his book *Building a Career in Software Engineering : a Comprehensive Guide to Success in the Software Industry,* has a lot to say about the career turnover in software engineering. For context, Heller writes that, “today, changing jobs every two years isn’t too unusual; four years is a generous stay, and a one-year stint is no problem at all on a resume” (*Building a Career in Software Engineering*). This idea of changing jobs so frequently is one that challenges the age-old idea that if one takes care of the company they work for, then their company will take care of them. Heller states that that kind of employee-employer relationship is entirely nonexistent in software engineering, and states exactly, “You don’t owe your company your personal loyalty— they certainly don’t see themselves as owing you any” (*Building a Career in Software Engineering*) Heller advises that an engineer’s relationship with their employer is strictly professional, and that one should maintain a professional relationship with their employer even past termination, so as to maintain valuable mediums of networking for the future. Heller also describes the primary reason for software engineers’ reasons to hop from one company to another when he writes that, “The allure of engineering is precisely its constant learning and discovery, which means engineers usually crave novelty” (*Building a Career in Software Engineering*). So, it is not usually because engineers are unhappy with their workplace that they find another job, but because the kind of person that becomes a software engineer takes pleasure in the challenge of learning new things and discovering their limits. Additionally, as aforementioned, the field of software engineering is constantly changing, and as Heller puts it, “a complacent engineer is an obsolete engineer” (*Building a Career in Software Engineering*). This frequent challenging of oneself allows one to arm themselves with an impressive arsenal of tactics and methodologies that they can use to tackle future problems in their career. A good software engineer adapts with their field, and it is through this way of thinking that an engineer can obtain a fruitful career in software engineering.
